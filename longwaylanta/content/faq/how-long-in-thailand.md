Two separate clocks run, and the bike's is shorter than yours.

## The bike's clock

The Foreign Vehicle Permit (FVP), issued by the Department of Land Transport, is
the binding one. Applications go through a Thai travel agency and should be
submitted at least five working days before entry, with a maximum of 30 days per
permit and not exceeding 60 days per calendar year. You can apply for two
consecutive FVPs before you arrive, and customs will often then issue a 60-day
temporary import permit at the border, so you skip the mid-trip customs office
visit.

Note that the FVP becomes void the moment you ride out of Thailand, so a hop
into Malaysia burns whatever days are left on it.

The Temporary Import Permit (TIP), issued by Customs, is the second document. It
is extendable 30 days at a time, free, at any customs house in the country, but
never beyond your visa expiry. On paper Customs can extend a vehicle's stay to
six months, and beyond that to eight in genuine breakdown or accident cases. In
practice the FVP's calendar-year cap arrives first.

Overstaying runs 1,000 baht per day up to a 10,000 baht ceiling, which is
annoying rather than ruinous, but it puts a black mark on a carnet you want
cleanly stamped.

## The trick worth planning around

The 60-day cap is per calendar year, not per rolling twelve months. Arrive in
November, ride out your 60 days through December, and the counter resets on
1 January for a fresh 60. That gives you roughly four months of legal riding
wrapped around a single Andaman dry season, which is exactly the Lanta window
(November to April). Our route timing already lands us in northern Thailand
around October or November, so this falls out almost for free.

## Your clock

Dutch passports currently get 60 days visa-exempt plus a 30-day extension. Be
aware that the Thai Cabinet approved ending the 60-day scheme on 19 May 2026,
moving most nationalities to 30 days, pending Royal Gazette publication, so
check the state of play closer to departure.

Given we're working remotely anyway, the DTV is the obvious fit: five years,
180 days per entry. It also lifts the visa ceiling on TIP extensions, so the
FVP becomes the only real constraint.

One caveat: FVP practice varies by border and by agent, and riders regularly
report the requirement appearing at one crossing and not another. Line up the
agency and both permits before reaching the Laos border rather than assuming it
can be sorted on the day.
