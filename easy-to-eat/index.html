a scoring system

Easy to Eat

A number from 0 to 10 for how much trouble a food gives you between picking it up and forgetting you ate it. Ten is an M&M. Zero is a lobster. Everything else is arithmetic.

The scale

Every food starts at 10 — perfectly, frictionlessly edible — and loses points for each thing it makes you do. Nothing ever earns points back, so the number only goes one way.

0 · a whole production 10 · effortless

A pit to park somewhere, a peel to drop, one wipe of the fingers. Still one-handed.

7–6

Fine at a desk if you lean forward. You'll be brushing something off your shirt afterwards.

5–4

Sit down for it

It wants a plate, a surface, and most of your attention. You are eating, not doing something else while eating.

3–2

An event, not a snack

Tools, technique, and a growing pile of remains. Someone has to clear the table afterwards.

1–0

Bring a bib

Special equipment, a learned method, a table you don't mind ruining, and a wash before you speak to anyone.

How we score

Five things can go wrong when you eat something, and they go wrong in order — before the first bite, during it, and after the last one. Each one costs 0, 1 or 2 points. Add the penalties, subtract from 10, and that's the score.

before

1 · Tools

What has to be in your hand before any food reaches your mouth.

−0Your hands, and only your hands. Nothing to fetch, nothing to wash up.
−1One ordinary utensil. A fork, a spoon, chopsticks — or a plate you can't put down.
−2A kit. Two implements working together, or something you'd have to go out and buy: a crab cracker, a lobster pick, corn holders, a bib.
during

2 · Effort

The work between "it's in front of me" and "it's swallowed" — peeling, cutting, prying, twirling, chewing.

−0Pick it up, chew twice, done. No preparation between you and the bite.
−1A small ritual per bite — a peel, a fold, a cut, a twirl — or genuine jaw work.
−2Sustained labour at a bad ratio. A minute of prying for two grams of food, or a technique somebody had to teach you.
during

3 · Mess

What lands on you, and on everything within a metre of you, while you're eating.

−0Clean throughout. You could do this in a white shirt while holding somebody's passport.
−1Crumbs, or fingertips you'd rather wipe. Survivable with a napkin.
−2You will have to wash. Grease, drips, squirt, or a stain that outlives the meal.
during

4 · Debris

The part that isn't food, and is now your responsibility.

−0All of it goes in. Nothing is left over and nothing comes back out.
−1One tidy remainder — a pit, a peel, a wrapper, a stick — that you can set down and forget about.
−2A pile. Shells, bones, husks, pips — or things you keep having to take back out of your mouth.
after

5 · Aftermath

What is still true about you ten minutes later.

−0No evidence. Nobody could tell.
−1Noticeable to you. Something wedged in a tooth, a faint smell on your fingers.
−2Noticeable to other people. Breath, stained lips or fingers, a smell in your clothes. You need a mirror before your next conversation.

Five axes, two points each, so the worst possible food loses ten and lands on zero. The scale can't go negative — which is the only reason lobster and durian are allowed to share a floor.

The fine print

Six rules that settle most of the arguments.

  1. Score it as served, not as it could be

    A pomegranate somebody else has already deseeded into a tub is a different food from a pomegranate. Score what actually lands in front of you, in the state it lands in.

  2. One normal portion, eaten normally

    Not the way a competitive eater would, not the way a four-year-old would. A single serving, by an adult who has eaten this kind of thing before.

  3. Taste is not a factor

    Easy is not the same as good. A rice cake outscores almost everything worth eating. The number measures friction, not pleasure, and the two are frequently opposites — which is roughly the whole point.

  4. Penalties don't stack inside an axis

    A chicken wing leaves bones and a greasy wrapper and a little pot of sauce. That is still a 2 for Debris, not a 4. Each axis caps at two, which is what keeps the scale honest.

  5. Context is a modifier, not part of the score

    A food's score is context-free — it's a property of the food. Then subtract up to 2 more for where you are: standing up, in a car, in the dark, or with something already in your other hand. That's a separate number, and it isn't the food's fault.

  6. When in doubt, use the laptop test

    Could you eat this at a laptop, in a white shirt, halfway through a meeting? A 10 is yes without thinking about it. A 5 is you'd close the laptop. A 0 is you'd leave the building.
